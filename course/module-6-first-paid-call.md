# Module 6: Your first paid call

**Time:** 2–3 hours
**You will leave with:** a price, a booking link, and a plan for running the call.

## The idea

You are not starting an agency. You are offering one thing: a conversation with someone who has seen the problem before. Keep the business as small as the offer.

That means four decisions and nothing more:

1. What exactly you sell
2. What it costs
3. How people book and pay
4. What happens on the call and after it

### 1. What you sell: one session, clearly bounded

A good first offer:

> **The [situation] session.** 60 minutes by video. You bring your real situation; I tell you what I see, what tends to happen next, and what I would check. You get a one-page written summary within two days.

One session, one price. No packages, retainers or proposals yet. Those can come once people ask for more.

### 2. What it costs

Three inputs:

- What people told you in Module 5 when you asked what would seem fair
- What the problem costs them if it goes wrong
- What an hour of your time is worth to you

A sensible starting range for a first advisory session is **$150 to $250 for 60 minutes**. Treat it as a starting point and adjust for your field; a problem with large financial or legal consequences supports more. Choose a figure, say it plainly, and do not apologise for it.

Two rules:

- **Do not work for free beyond the first conversation.** Free advice is valued at its price.
- **Raise the price after every three paid sessions** until someone hesitates.

### 3. Booking and payment

You need two links:

| Need | Simple option |
|---|---|
| Choosing a time | A scheduling tool such as Calendly: you set your available hours, they pick a slot |
| Paying | A payment link from Stripe or PayPal, or a scheduling tool that takes payment at booking |

Payment at the time of booking is simplest. It avoids invoices and ensures people turn up.

### 4. Staying advisory

"Advisory" means you give your view and they decide and do the work. It protects you and keeps the work light.

- You **do**: listen, share patterns, review what they have prepared, suggest what to check.
- You **do not**: do the work for them, sign anything, make their decisions, or give regulated professional advice you are no longer licensed to give.

Put this sentence on your page and in your confirmation email:

> This session shares my experience and opinion to help you think through your situation. It is not [legal / medical / financial / engineering] advice, and decisions remain yours.

Three practical points:

- **Tax.** Income from advising is usually taxable and may affect a pension or benefits. Speak to an accountant before your first payment, not after.
- **Insurance.** Ask an insurance broker whether professional liability cover makes sense for your field.
- **Old agreements.** Reread any confidentiality or non-compete terms from former employers.

## Watch it happen

Linda decides on one offer: *The Audit Readiness Session*, 60 minutes, $200, with a one-page written summary afterwards. Two of her three practice conversations had said they would have paid "a couple of hundred".

She sets up a scheduling tool with Tuesday and Thursday afternoons available, connects payment, and replaces the email button on her web page with "Book the Audit Readiness Session, $200".

Marcus books the following week. Before the call she asks him by email for three things: what he wants to walk away with, the one document he is most unsure about with identifying details removed, and his deadline.

On the call she follows the same shape as before, for longer. He shows what he has found since they last spoke. She tells him what it reminds her of, and what she would have ready for the auditors. He decides what to do.

Afterwards she dictates her notes to her assistant and asks for a one-page summary: what he described, what she observed, three things to check, and what to do if the answer is bad. She edits it and sends it the next morning with a line at the bottom: "If it would help to talk again after the audit, here is the link."

He replies that afternoon. His manager wants to know if she would do the same for two others on the team.

## Your turn

### Step 1. Define the session (20 minutes)

```
Help me describe a single paid advisory session. My one-sentence
offer: [paste]. My framework: [paste]. What people said was most
useful in practice conversations: [paste].

Write: a name for the session, a three-sentence description of what
happens, a list of what the client should bring, and what they
receive afterwards. Plain language. Under 120 words.
```

### Step 2. Set your price (15 minutes)

Write down one number. Say it aloud three times: "The session is [price]." If you cannot say it without adding "but I could do less", practise until you can.

### Step 3. Set up booking and payment (45 minutes)

1. Open an account with a scheduling tool.
2. Create one event: your session name, 60 minutes, video call.
3. Set the hours you are willing to take calls. Protect your retirement: two afternoons a week is plenty.
4. Turn on payment at booking, or add a payment link to the confirmation message.
5. Book a test session yourself to see what your client will see.
6. Replace the button on your web page and one-pager with the booking link.

### Step 4. Write your three emails (30 minutes)

```
Write three short emails for my advisory session, in a warm and plain
voice:

1. Confirmation: sent on booking. Thanks them, says what to bring,
   asks them to reply with what they most want to walk away with, and
   includes this sentence: "This session shares my experience and
   opinion to help you think through your situation. It is not
   [type] advice, and decisions remain yours."
2. Follow-up: sent within two days, with the written summary
   attached, and an invitation to book again.
3. Check-in: sent three weeks later, asking how it went and whether
   they know anyone else facing the same situation.

Session details: [paste].
```

### Step 5. Plan the call (20 minutes)

| Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Welcome. Confirm what they want from the hour |
| 5–20 | Their situation. You ask; they show |
| 20–40 | What you see. Patterns, what tends to happen next |
| 40–50 | Options and what to check |
| 50–60 | They state their next step. You say what the summary will cover |

Afterwards, for the summary:

```
Here are my rough notes from an advisory call: [paste, with names
removed]. Turn them into a one-page summary for the client with four
headings: What you described, What I observed, Three things to check,
If the answer is bad. Use plain language and keep my wording.
```

Read every line before sending. It goes out under your name.

### Step 6. Invite your first paying client (15 minutes)

Write to each person from your Module 5 conversations, and to anyone who responded to your post:

> Thank you again for the conversation. I've now set this up as a proper session: 60 minutes on [situation], with a written summary afterwards, for [price]. If it would be useful to you or to someone on your team, here's the link: [link].

Record everything on the [first call worksheet](worksheets/6-first-call-planner.md).

## Done when

- [ ] One session defined, with a name and a price
- [ ] Booking link live, tested, and on your web page
- [ ] Three emails written
- [ ] The "not professional advice" sentence is on your page and in your confirmation
- [ ] At least three people invited to book
- [ ] You have spoken to, or booked a time with, an accountant

## Bring to the call

Your price, said out loud. And your booking link.

## What comes next

When three people have paid you, you have a small consulting practice. From there:

- Raise the price.
- Build a second framework from another story in your bank.
- Ask each client who else should see your checklist.
- Offer a manager a session for their whole team.

You do not need to do more than that unless you want to.

## If you get stuck

| Problem | Try this |
|---|---|
| The price feels too high | Compare it with what the problem costs when it goes wrong |
| Nobody books in the first fortnight | Send the checklist to ten more people. Volume of personal messages is the lever |
| Someone asks you to do the work for them | "I advise; I don't do the work. I'm happy to review what you produce." |
| Someone wants ongoing help | Offer a block of three sessions at the same hourly price |
