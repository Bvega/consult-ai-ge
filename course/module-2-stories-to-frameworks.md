# Module 2: From stories to frameworks

**Time:** 2–3 hours
**You will leave with:** one named framework and a checklist somebody else could use on Monday morning.

## The idea

A story is yours. A framework is something another person can pick up and use without you in the room. Turning one into the other is what makes your experience sellable.

"Framework" sounds grander than it is. It means a small, named, repeatable tool. The most useful shapes are simple:

| Shape | Example | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Warning signs** | "3 signs your budget is hiding a problem" | Things people miss until too late |
| **Checklist** | "Before you sign a supplier contract: 7 checks" | Tasks done under pressure |
| **Questions to ask** | "5 questions to ask in your first week as a manager" | Situations that need judgment |
| **Steps** | "The first 48 hours after a failed inspection" | Crises and handovers |
| **Either/or guide** | "Repair or replace? How to decide" | Recurring decisions |

Notice that every example names a specific moment in somebody's working life. That is deliberate. A person searching for help does not type "operational wisdom". They type the problem that is keeping them awake.

### The test of a good framework

- **Specific.** It names a situation a real person is in right now.
- **Short.** Three to seven items. More than seven and nobody remembers it.
- **Observable.** Each item is something you can see or check, not a feeling.
- **Earned.** Each item comes from something that happened to you. If you cannot tell the story behind an item, cut it.

The fourth test is what separates you from an AI tool or a textbook. Anyone can generate a list. Only you can say "I have seen this, and here is what happened next."

## Watch it happen

Linda looks at her five stories and picks the audit one. It has three clear signals, and she knows younger analysts dread audits.

She asks the assistant to find the pattern. It suggests a list of eight "best practices for budget oversight". Linda reads it and types:

> No. This reads like a textbook. I want only the three things I actually noticed, written as warning signs someone could spot in their own department.

The second attempt is closer. She rewrites each sign in her own words, and for each one adds a line beginning "What I'd do", because a warning without an action just makes people anxious.

**3 Warning Signs Your Department Budget Is Hiding a Problem**

1. **The reports are always late, and there is always a good reason.** One late report is life. Three in a row is a signal. *What I'd do: ask to see the working file, not the finished report.*
2. **The forecast is too smooth.** Real spending is lumpy. The same small variance every month means someone is managing the number, not reporting it. *What I'd do: compare the forecast to actual invoices for one month.*
3. **Questions about numbers get answered with stories about workload.** When "why is this line over?" gets "we've been so short-staffed", the person does not know or does not want to say. *What I'd do: ask the same question in writing and ask for a figure.*

Then she tests it. She imagines an analyst two years into the job and asks herself whether that person could use this tomorrow without calling her. Yes. Would they still want to call her? Also yes, because spotting a sign is the easy part and knowing what to do about a colleague who is hiding something is not. That gap is her consulting offer.

## Your turn

### Step 1. Choose your story (15 minutes)

Look at your story bank and pick the one where:

- the signal is clearest
- the situation still happens to people in your field today
- you would enjoy talking about it for an hour

If you cannot decide, type:

```
Here are the titles and signals of my five stories: [paste them].
Which one describes a problem that people early in their careers in
[field] most often face and worry about? Give your reasoning, then
ask me which one I'd most enjoy discussing with a younger colleague.
```

### Step 2. Find the pattern (30 minutes)

Paste the full story and type:

```
Here is a story from my career: [paste story].

Find the pattern in it that someone else could reuse. Do not add
general best practices. Use only what is in my story.

Suggest which shape fits best: warning signs, a checklist, questions
to ask, steps, or an either/or guide. Then draft it with between three
and five items. Each item must be something a person can observe or
check.
```

### Step 3. Make it yours (45 minutes)

This is the step that matters. For each item:

1. Rewrite it in the words you would use out loud.
2. Add one line: "What I'd do" or "Why it matters".
3. Ask: can I tell the story behind this? If not, delete it.

If the assistant has slipped in anything you did not live, take it out.

### Step 4. Name it (15 minutes)

A good name states the number, the form and the situation: *"3 Warning Signs Your Department Budget Is Hiding a Problem."* Plain beats clever.

```
Suggest ten titles for this framework. Each should include the number
of items and name the specific situation the reader is in. No puns,
no jargon. Here is the framework: [paste].
```

### Step 5. Pressure-test it (20 minutes)

```
Act as a sceptical 28-year-old [junior role] who is good with
software but has never been through [the situation]. Read my
framework and tell me: what is unclear, what would you not know how
to act on, and what question would you want to ask me afterwards?
```

Fix what is unclear. Keep a note of the questions it says it would ask. Those questions are what people will pay to discuss with you.

Record the result on the [framework worksheet](worksheets/2-framework-builder.md).

## Done when

- [ ] One framework with three to seven items
- [ ] A name that states the number and the situation
- [ ] Every item is observable and has a "what I'd do" line
- [ ] You can tell the story behind every item
- [ ] You have a list of at least three questions a reader would want to ask you

## Bring to the call

Read your framework aloud. The group will tell you which item landed hardest.

## If you get stuck

| Problem | Try this |
|---|---|
| The framework sounds generic | Go back to the story and find the detail only you would know |
| You have twelve items | Ask: "If they could only remember three, which three?" |
| Every item is a feeling ("trust your gut") | Ask yourself what you saw that produced the gut feeling. That is the item |
| You think it is too obvious | It is obvious to you after thirty years. That is the point |
