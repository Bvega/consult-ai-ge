# Case study: Linda and Marcus

Linda and Marcus are an illustration invented for this course. They are not real people, and their results are not a promise. They show what each module produces when it is done in order.

## Who they are

**Linda** spent 31 years in hospital finance, twelve as a finance director. She led four external audits. She retired two years ago, misses the work but not the hours, and has never used an AI tool.

**Marcus** is 28, a hospital analyst. He builds excellent dashboards and has never been through an audit. His first is three weeks away.

## Week by week

| Week | What Linda did | What she had at the end |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Opened an AI assistant and talked about her career for ten minutes | Confidence that she would not break anything |
| 1 | Was interviewed by the assistant about the worst week of her career | Five situations, including *The audit that found what the late reports were hiding* |
| 2 | Rejected a generic list of "best practices" and kept only what she had seen herself | *3 Warning Signs Your Department Budget Is Hiding a Problem* |
| 3 | Put the three signs into a template and published a one-page site | A PDF and a web page, built in an afternoon |
| 4 | Replaced "Retired" in her headline with what she helps with | A one-sentence offer, one post, ten personal messages |
| 5 | Held three unpaid conversations, including one with Marcus | The words her readers use, and a price range |
| 6 | Set up one session at $200 with booking and payment | A paid call with Marcus, and a request from his manager |

## The framework

**3 Warning Signs Your Department Budget Is Hiding a Problem**

*For hospital finance analysts in their first few years.*

1. **The reports are always late, and there is always a good reason.**
   One late report is life. Three in a row is a signal.
   *What I'd do: ask to see the working file, not the finished report.*

2. **The forecast is too smooth.**
   Real spending is lumpy. The same small variance every month means someone is managing the number, not reporting it.
   *What I'd do: compare the forecast with actual invoices for one month.*

3. **Questions about numbers get answered with explanations about workload.**
   When "why is this line over?" gets "we've been so short-staffed", the person does not know or does not want to say.
   *What I'd do: ask the same question in writing and ask for a figure.*

## The offer

> I help hospital finance analysts who are facing their first external audit to spot problems before the auditors do, drawing on 31 years in hospital finance and four audits led as director.

**The Audit Readiness Session.** 60 minutes by video, $200. Bring your real situation with identifying details removed. Receive a one-page written summary within two days.

## What made it work

- **She started from a real situation.** The three signs came from something that happened to her.
- **She overruled the AI.** Twice it offered polished, generic text. Twice she said no and put her own words back.
- **She went narrow.** "Analysts facing their first audit" was a small group, and one of them recognised himself.
- **She asked before she told.** Marcus showed his dashboard first. Her observation landed because it was about his numbers.
- **He found her through a person.** The checklist reached Marcus because a manager forwarded it.

## What AI did and did not do

| AI did | AI did not |
|---|---|
| Ask the follow-up question that surfaced the early signs | Know what the early signs were |
| Turn her spoken account into tidy paragraphs | Decide which details mattered |
| Draft the text for the one-pager | Lead four audits |
| Play a sceptical junior so she could rehearse | Notice the line on Marcus's dashboard |
| Turn call notes into a summary | Take responsibility for the advice |
