# Module 5: Work across generations

**Time:** 2–3 hours
**You will leave with:** three real conversations with younger professionals, and a way of working with them that both sides enjoy.

## The idea

The people you want to help grew up with tools you find unfamiliar. They build a dashboard in an afternoon. They also have not yet watched a project fail slowly, or sat across from someone who was hiding bad news.

That is a trade, and a fair one:

| They bring | You bring |
|---|---|
| Speed with tools and data | Knowing which number to distrust |
| Energy and current methods | Pattern recognition: "I've seen this before" |
| Fluency with AI and software | Knowing what happens next |
| Fresh eyes | How to say a hard thing to a senior person |

The work goes well when each side does its own half. It goes badly when you try to out-tool them or when you lecture.

### Five habits of advisors people come back to

1. **Ask before you tell.** Start with "What have you tried?" and "What are you worried will happen?" You will give better advice, and they will be readier to hear it.
2. **Offer patterns, not orders.** "When I've seen this, what usually happens next is…" lets them decide. "What you need to do is…" invites resistance.
3. **Be curious about their tools.** Ask them to show you how they built the thing. You will learn something, and they will remember that you asked.
4. **Drop the nostalgia.** "Back in my day" ends the conversation. The principle survives; the anecdote about fax machines does not.
5. **Admit what has changed.** "The regulation is different now, so check that part. The human behaviour is the same." That sentence builds more trust than any credential.

### How a good session runs

A simple shape for any conversation, paid or not:

1. **Their situation** (10 minutes). They talk. You ask questions.
2. **The pattern** (10 minutes). You say what it reminds you of and what tends to happen.
3. **Their options** (5 minutes). Two or three ways forward, with what you would watch for in each.
4. **Next step** (5 minutes). They say what they will do. You offer to look at it afterwards.

You talk for less than half of it.

## Watch it happen

Marcus is 28, a hospital analyst, very good with dashboards and three weeks away from his first external audit. A manager forwarded him Linda's checklist. He recognises sign number two in his own department's forecast and sends her a message.

They talk for thirty minutes, unpaid. Linda does not begin with advice. She asks what he is seeing. He shares his screen and shows her a dashboard that would have taken her old team a fortnight to produce. She says so, and asks how he built it.

Then she points at one line. "That variance is the same every month. In my experience that is not good news. Who prepares that figure?"

He had not thought to ask. She tells him, briefly, what happened the time she did not ask either. She does not tell him what to do. She tells him the two ways it tends to go and what she would want to know before the auditors arrive.

At the end he asks if he can come back after he has spoken to the department. She says yes, and that the next one would be a paid session. He says of course.

Linda writes down three things afterwards: the exact words Marcus used to describe his worry, the question he asked that she had not expected, and the fact that he found the checklist through his manager, not through her post.

## Your turn

### Step 1. Rehearse with the assistant (30 minutes)

Practising first makes the real conversation easier.

```
Role-play with me. You are [name], a [age]-year-old [job title] who is
skilled with software and data but has never been through [the
situation my framework covers]. You are a little nervous and a little
sceptical of advice from someone who retired before the current tools
existed. I am an experienced advisor meeting you for the first time.

Stay in character. Respond the way that person realistically would.
After about ten exchanges, step out of character and tell me: where I
asked good questions, where I lectured, and one thing I could do
differently.
```

Do it twice. The second time, try to speak for less than half of the conversation.

### Step 2. Find three people (30 minutes)

You are looking for three people early in their careers in your field. Sources, in order of ease:

1. Replies to last week's ten messages
2. Former colleagues who manage younger staff: "Is there anyone on your team who'd find a half-hour on [situation] useful?"
3. Your professional association's mentoring scheme or younger-members group
4. Family and friends' children who work in your field

The invitation:

> I'm developing a short advisory session on [situation] for people early in their careers. I'd value thirty minutes of your time to try it out: you bring a real situation, I'll share what I've seen, and you tell me honestly whether it was useful. No charge.

### Step 3. Hold three conversations (30 minutes each)

Use the four-part shape above. Bring your framework but do not read it out. Let their situation lead.

At the end, ask three things:

1. "What was most useful?"
2. "What did you hope I'd cover that I didn't?"
3. "Would you have paid for that? What would have seemed fair?"

The third question feels awkward. Ask it anyway. The answers will set your price in Module 6.

### Step 4. Capture what you learned (20 minutes)

After each conversation, note on the [conversation worksheet](worksheets/5-conversation-notes.md):

- The words they used for their problem
- What they found most useful
- What surprised you
- What they said about paying

Then:

```
Here are my notes from three conversations with younger
professionals: [paste]. What phrases did they use for their problem
that I should use in my own description? What did they value most?
What should I change about my framework or my one-sentence offer?
```

Update your one-pager and offer if the answer is clear.

## If you get stuck

| Problem | Try this |
|---|---|
| You cannot find three people | Two is enough to go on with. Ask on the call; another member may know someone |
| They did all the talking | Good. That is a successful session |
| They knew more than you about part of it | Say so, and ask them to explain. Then offer what they do not have |
| The conversation drifted into general career advice | Fine once. Next time, open by naming the situation you are there to discuss |
